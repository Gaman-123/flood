Spatio-Temporal Multi-Pollutant Exposure-Aware
Routing with Personalized Health Impact
Estimation for Urban Mobility
Fathima Mehnaz1, Saanvi Rai 1, Sheza Rizqin 1, Tasvi Rai 1, Rithika Shettigar 1
1 Dept. of Computer Science & Engineering
Sahyadri College of Engineering and Management
Mangaluru, India
Abstract—Urban commuters are persistently exposed to het-
erogeneous mixtures of atmospheric pollutants whose concen-
trations vary sharply across road segments and throughout the
day. Conventional navigation systems optimize routing solely for
time or distance, rendering them ill-suited for health-conscious
mobility planning. This paper presents a spatio-temporal, multi-
pollutant exposure-aware routing framework that integrates
Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) temporal forecasting, Or-
dinary Kriging spatial interpolation, and road-network graph
modeling to estimate segment-level pollutant concentrations for
six species—PM2.5, PM10, CO, NO 2, O 3, and SO 2. These
estimates are aggregated into an Air Exposure Risk Index (AERI)
that drives a multi-objective route optimizer balancing travel effi-
ciency against cumulative exposure burden. A personalized phys-
iological impact module translates route-level risk differentials
into interpretable health-trend indicators calibrated to individual
baseline profiles. Preliminary feasibility experiments across 60
simulated source–destination pairs demonstrate a mean AERI
reduction of 24.6% with a travel-time overhead of only 8.7%,
alongside single-step LSTM prediction errors of 5.8 µg/m3 MAE
for PM2.5. The framework constitutes a practical foundation for
health-aware smart-city navigation platforms serving vulnerable
population groups.

I. I NTRODUCTION
Urbanization has intensified the co-location of dense vehic-
ular traffic and human activity, producing chronic short-term
and long-term exposure to a complex mixture of atmospheric
pollutants. The World Health Organization estimates that 99%
of the global population breathes air exceeding WHO guide-
line limits, contributing to approximately 7 million premature
deaths annually [1]. While smart-city initiatives have produced
rich networks of fixed-station and low-cost mobile air quality
sensors [2], the vast majority of navigation systems remain
indifferent to real-time pollution landscapes, routing users
through heavily polluted arterials when comparably efficient
alternatives exist.
The challenge is threefold. First, pollutant concentrations
are spatially heterogeneous at sub-kilometer resolution, ex-
hibiting markedly different behavior on adjacent streets de-
pending on traffic density, building morphology, and prevailing
wind [3]. Second, concentrations are temporally dynamic:
rush-hour peaks, photochemical noon maxima for ozone, and
nocturnal inversions for particulate matter demand predictive,
not merely reactive, modeling [4]. Third, health susceptibility
is individually variable: individuals with asthma, chronic ob-
structive pulmonary disease (COPD), or cardiovascular con-
ditions face disproportionate risk from the same exposure
levels [5], [6].
Prior work in health-aware routing has addressed subsets of
these dimensions [7]–[9], but no single framework integrates
temporal prediction, spatial interpolation, multi-pollutant com-
posite scoring, multi-objective route optimization, and person-
alized physiological risk estimation in an end-to-end pipeline.
This paper addresses that gap.
Contributions
The principal contributions of this work are as follows.
• Multi-pollutant AERI formulation. A composite Air
Exposure Risk Index aggregating six pollutant species
(PM2.5, PM10, CO, NO 2, O 3, SO 2) via normalized,
health-weight-informed scores.
• Spatio-temporal hybrid estimator. An LSTM–Kriging
fusion model that produces near-future, road-segment-
level concentration estimates from sparse fixed-station
data.
• Multi-objective routing. A Dijkstra-based optimizer on
an OSMnx road graph that trades travel time against
cumulative AERI exposure, parameterized by a user-
controlled health-priority scalar λ.
• Personalized physiological module. A baseline-sensitive
impact layer that converts AERI route differentials into
interpretable health-trend indicators (heart rate, blood
pressure, SpO 2, HRV).
• Interactive web prototype. A Flask/Leaflet dashboard
demonstrating end-to-end route visualization, risk over-
lay, and personalized health impact cards.

II. R ELATED WORK
A. Health-Aware and Exposure-Minimizing Routing
Early work by Nykiforuk et al. [10] demonstrated that route
choice substantially mediates pedestrian exposure to traffic-
related pollution. Subsequent systems incorporated static pol-
lution maps into graph-based planners: Steinle et al. [7] used
spatial regression to derive road-segment NO 2 estimates for
bicycle routing, while Hong et al. [8] proposed a multi-
objective shortest-path formulation balancing distance and
particle exposure for pedestrians. Nyhan et al. [9] leveraged
mobile sensing on taxis to construct empirical exposure maps
for Manhattan. These approaches treat pollution as static,
neglecting temporal drift and limiting applicability to pre-
measured corridors. Our work extends this line by adding real-
time temporal forecasting, making recommendations forward-
looking rather than retrospective.
B. Air Quality Forecasting with Deep Learning
Recurrent sequence models have become dominant in pol-
lutant time-series forecasting. Zhao et al. [11] applied stacked
LSTMs to predict PM2.5 at Beijing stations, achieving sub-
10 µg/m3 MAE at one-hour horizons. Liang et al. [12]
extended this to a spatial attention mechanism (STDN) that
couples meteorological covariates with pollutant sequences.
Transformer-based architectures, including the Informer [13]
and PatchTST [14], have demonstrated competitive accuracy
with lower training cost on multivariate environmental series.
Li et al. [15] deployed a graph neural network (GNN) over
a sensor network, exploiting topological dependencies among
monitoring stations. We adopt a single-station LSTM base-
line in the present work, acknowledging that graph-based or
attention-augmented architectures represent a natural exten-
sion.
C. Spatial Interpolation of Atmospheric Pollutants
Geostatistical methods, particularly Kriging, have long
served as the gold standard for continuous surface estima-
tion from sparse observations [16]. Ordinary Kriging (OK)
minimizes the mean squared prediction error under station-
arity assumptions [17], while Universal Kriging incorporates
spatial trend terms. Berrocal et al. [18] fused determinis-
tic photochemical model output with Kriging residuals to
improve PM 2.5 estimation. Machine learning alternatives—
random forests with spatial lags [19] and spatial autoen-
coders [20]—offer flexibility at the cost of interpretability. We
choose OK for its closed-form uncertainty estimates, which
could later be propagated into route-level confidence bounds.
D. Urban Road Network Modeling
Boeing [21] introduced OSMnx, which has become the de
facto tool for extracting, analyzing, and visualizing Open-
StreetMap road networks in Python. Its NetworkX backend en-
ables graph-theoretic algorithms including weighted shortest-
path computation [22], while its built-in graph simplification
preserves topological accuracy. Recent extensions integrate
turn-restriction modeling and elevation-grade penalties [23],

E. Personalized Health and Wearable Integration
Wearable devices have enabled continuous monitoring of
physiological proxies such as heart rate, blood oxygen sat-
uration (SpO 2), and heart rate variability (HRV) [24]. Epi-
demiological studies quantify associations between short-term
pollution spikes and acute cardiovascular or respiratory re-
sponses [5], [25]. Digital health routing platforms such as
AirCasting [26] and BreatheSafe [27] link wearable biometric
streams to route suggestions, but typically use single-pollutant
thresholds and do not perform forward-looking physiological
projection. Our personalized health module operationalizes
these epidemiological relationships as a relative risk-trend
layer for interpretable user feedback, while explicitly discour-
aging clinical overinterpretation.
III. S YSTEM ARCHITECTURE
The proposed framework is organized into six modular
layers, as depicted in Fig. 1. Each layer exposes a clean
interface, enabling independent upgrading of individual com-
ponents without retraining or redesigning the pipeline.
Layer 1 — Data Ingestion. Pollutant observations for
PM2.5, PM10, CO, NO2, O3, and SO2 are fetched from public
air quality APIs (e.g., OpenAQ, CPCB, IQAir) at configurable
polling intervals. Ingested records undergo outlier screening
via interquartile range (IQR) flagging, unit normalization, and
temporal alignment to a common 1-hour grid.
Layer 2 — Temporal Modeling. An LSTM network fore-
casts each pollutant’s concentration at the next time step (t+1)
conditioned on a sliding window of historical observations.
A multi-output variant can predict all six species jointly,
capturing inter-pollutant correlation (Section IV-B).
Layer 3 — Spatial Modeling. Ordinary Kriging (OK)
maps sparse station observations to a continuous spatial field
at the query time. Road segment midpoints are sampled
from this field to obtain spatially interpolated concentrations
(Section IV-C).
Layer 4 — Road Graph Layer. OSMnx extracts the
drivable road network for the city bounding box as a directed
multigraph G = ( V, E). Edge weights are initialized with
travel-time estimates derived from posted speed limits and
segment lengths.
Layer 5 — Risk & Routing Layer. The AERI composite
score is computed for each edge and fused with travel-time
cost into a multi-objective edge weight. A modified Dijkstra
algorithm [28] identifies the Pareto-optimal safer route (Sec-
tion IV-E).
Layer 6 — Health Impact Layer. The AERI differential
between the direct and safer routes is mapped to relative
physiological trend indicators (heart rate, systolic blood pres-
sure, SpO 2, HRV) for the authenticated user, whose medical
baseline is stored in a privacy-preserving profile store (Sec-
tion IV-F).

F . Personalized Physiological Impact Module
The health impact module translates route-level AERI dif-
ferentials into interpretable physiological trend indicators. Let
∆E = E(Rdirect) − E(R∗) denote the exposure reduction
achieved by the safer route. For a user with baseline physiolog-
ical profile b = [ HR0, SBP0, SpO2,0, HRV0]⊤, the expected
trend magnitude for each biomarker k is modeled as:
∆ϕk = βk · ∆E · mk(b), (18)
where βk is a pollutant-sensitivity coefficient derived from
epidemiological literature [5], [33] and mk(b) is a baseline
vulnerability multiplier (e.g., users with pre-existing cardio-
vascular risk scores receive a larger mk). These estimates
represent relative trend indicators for route comparison and
are explicitly not intended as clinical diagnostic outputs.
V. I MPLEMENTATION
A. Technology Stack
The prototype is implemented as a Python 3.10 Flask
web application [34]. The Leaflet.js library [35] renders the
interactive map with dynamically colored road overlays. Road
network data are retrieved via OSMnx 5.0 [21] backed by the
Overpass API. Pollutant data are ingested from the OpenAQ
v2 REST API [36]. The LSTM model is implemented in
TensorFlow 2.13 [37] with the Keras high-level API. Spatial
interpolation uses the PyKrige library [38]. Route optimization
builds on NetworkX 3.1 [22].
B. Data Sources and Preprocessing
Hourly pollutant records are fetched for all available mon-
itoring stations within the study bounding box. Time-series
alignment is performed to a uniform UTC grid with linear
interpolation for gaps shorter than 3 hours; sequences with
larger gaps are excluded from training. Outliers exceeding
mean ±3σ are flagged and replaced by the station’s rolling
24-hour median. Spatial coordinates are projected to UTM for
distance calculations.
C. Model Configuration
The LSTM is trained for 50 epochs with the Adam op-
timizer [39] (learning rate η = 10 −3, batch size 64). In-
put sequences are standardized per-station using training-set
statistics. The variogram parameters for Kriging are fitted on
the same training period. The training, validation, and test
splits follow a temporal 70/15/15 partition to prevent data
leakage [40].

The 24.6% mean AERI reduction at 8.7% travel-time over-
head demonstrates that meaningful exposure mitigation is
achievable within practical mobility constraints. The Pareto
frontier traced by varying λ allows the system to serve a range
of user preferences: time-critical users ( λ → 1) recover near-
shortest routes, while health-prioritizing users ( λ → 0) gain
maximum exposure benefit at higher time cost. The asymme-
try of the exposure-reduction distribution—with a long right
tail toward 38%—suggests that geographic routing flexibility

B. Multi-Pollutant vs. Single-Pollutant Ranking
A key motivation for AERI over single-pollutant routing
is that NO 2 and O 3 spatial patterns diverge substantially
from PM 2.5: NO 2 peaks near arterial traffic; O 3 is often
highest downwind in cleaner residential zones due to titration
effects [42]. Routing on PM 2.5 alone may inadvertently route
users through high-O 3 residential streets, increasing ozone
exposure. The AERI composite captures this co-pollutant
tradeoff. Quantifying the magnitude of routing disagreement
between single- and multi-pollutant objectives across city
typologies is reserved for future work.
C. Limitations
Simulation basis. The present experiments are conducted
under simulated, synthetic-but-realistic pollution scenarios.
Replacement with multi-day measured datasets is the highest-
priority validation step.

VIII. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
This paper presented a spatio-temporal, multi-pollutant
exposure-aware routing framework for urban mobility. The
system integrates LSTM temporal forecasting, Ordinary Krig-
ing spatial interpolation, OSMnx road-network modeling,
and a composite multi-objective route optimizer to generate
health-aware route recommendations personalized to individ-
ual physiological baselines. Preliminary feasibility experi-
ments demonstrate a mean AERI exposure reduction of 24.6%
at a travel-time overhead of 8.7%, with LSTM prediction
errors competitive with published benchmarks.
Concrete directions for future work include:
• Real-data validation on multi-month measured datasets
from dense sensor deployments.
• Uncertainty quantification via Kriging variance prop-
agation and LSTM Monte Carlo dropout [43], enabling
confidence-annotated route suggestions.
• Graph neural network forecasting to exploit spatial
correlations among monitoring stations [15].
• Multimodal extension supporting pedestrian and cyclist
networks with elevation-grade and wind-direction adjust-
ments.
• Dynamic traffic fusion integrating live speed data from
OSRM or Google Maps API to improve travel-time
estimates.
• Demographic equity analysis to ensure routing rec-
ommendations do not systematically disadvantage un-
derserved communities through spatial bias in sensor
coverage.
• Clinical calibration of the physiological module in part-
nership with public health researchers to ground health-
trend indicators in validated exposure-response func-
tions [33].
The framework constitutes a principled and extensible foun-
dation for next-generation health-aware smart-city navigation
infrastructure.
